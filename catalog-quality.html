Engineering quality in: root-cause analysis and automation in Amazon catalog operations

Role
Senior Catalog Associate, also internal SME and certified Mentor

Dec 2021 – Nov 2025 (automation impact measured over 12 months)

The challenge

Our 25-member catalog team handled listing enrichment, product attributes and data fixes for marketplaces in the US, UK, Italy, France and Spain, working with stakeholder teams based in China.

Quality issues kept recurring, and they were mostly small human errors repeated at scale. In listing enrichment, these included grammar and punctuation mistakes, content exceeding word limits, weak paraphrasing, and forbidden words. In business performance reports, they included copy-paste mistakes, typos, and missing data in calculations. Each error was minor, but together they caused rework, duplicate effort and wasted capacity, and catching them depended entirely on careful manual checking.

My role

As a Senior Associate operating with minimal direction, I owned my end-to-end process and daily SLA, monitored KPIs, and looked for permanent fixes to recurring problems. Beyond my core work, I served as an internal Subject Matter Expert and a certified Mentor for new hires.

The approach

1. Finding the real cause

I started with data rather than assumptions. Using a histogram, I analysed the frequency of each error type alongside its nature and cause, which showed clearly where most defects were coming from. A root-cause analysis then confirmed that most were not caused by unclear guidelines or tool failures, but by predictable human errors in repetitive manual steps. Because the errors followed patterns, they could be prevented through automation instead of relying on manual review alone.

2. Building the case for automation

Automation needed buy-in, so I wrote a one-page proposal for my manager and the task owner, and walked the China-based SME through it. It covered:

  • the error identified and how often it occurred
  • why automation was needed
  • the expected impact on quality and headcount costs
  • how effectiveness would be measured, and the target outcome

3. From idea to team-wide tool

  • I designed a feasible solution and verified it across multiple products before proposing it
  • After approval, I worked with a technical team member to build a macro-based automated solution, covering automated quality checks on listing data and reducing manual steps in performance reporting
  • The tool worked alongside manual QC: associates still reviewed their work, while the macros caught repetitive errors such as copy-paste mistakes, typos and missed data
  • It was published internally and rolled out with training. It was adopted by our entire team for daily work and by the China-based team for quality assurance

4. Consistent delivery

Alongside improvement work, I maintained a 100% SLA and 99% accuracy as a stakeholder point of contact by planning my work daily, prioritising by deadline, data pipeline timing and operational windows, and running a self-quality check before every submission.

5. Faster onboarding through mentoring

As a certified mentor, I trained 3 batches of new hires (15 people). During the first batch, I noticed the standard week-one onboarding checklist included tasks that weren't relevant to our team, plus several repetitive steps. I worked with our trainer in China to build a team-specific training deck based on our actual day-to-day work, removing irrelevant tasks and consolidating repetitive ones. This cut onboarding time by 5.5 hours per new hire, and the deck became the standard onboarding material for all following batches.

Results

  • 25% fewer errors, measured over 12 months
  • 5% savings in headcount costs by eliminating duplicate work
  • Adopted across two teams: used by our catalog team for daily work and by the China-based team for QA
  • 100% SLA and 99% accuracy maintained
  • 15 new hires mentored across 3 batches; onboarding shortened by 5.5 hours per new hire, with the new deck adopted as the team's standard

Reflection

The most valuable step wasn't the automation itself but the one-page business case. Turning a frustration everyone noticed into data (how often, what it cost, what success would look like) is what got the solution approved, built and adopted.
