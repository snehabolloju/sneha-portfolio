From data to decisions: a CRM-based predictive analytics framework for a luxury jeweller

Role
Program Manager and author of the MBA final project (Business Analytics, BITS Pilani)
Timeline
Jan – Apr 2026, five phases from data preparation to final report, built alongside the CRM rollout
Scale
3 branches · Sales, Inventory, Orders and Leadership stakeholders · 6 vendor teams
100%adoption within 4 weeks
+8%conversion, Q1 2026
+15%customer retention through Q2 2026
+15%process efficiency
−10%operational errors

The challenge

The retailer's CRM was being used as a digital address book: it stored names, numbers and past purchases, but it did not tell anyone what to do next. Sales consultants decided whom to call from memory and instinct, follow-ups slipped, and buying occasions such as weddings, anniversaries and festivals passed without a conversation. When a senior consultant left, the client relationships and the knowledge behind them left too.

The data existed. What was missing was a way to turn it into action. The challenge got harder as the program grew, because each new initiative (referrals, digital gold, order tracking, leadership reporting) needed to read the same customer data in a different way.

My role

I designed the framework as my MBA final project while leading the CRM program, so it was built into the live system and not left as a paper exercise. I cleaned the data in Excel and built the RFM scoring and rule logic in Python. I worked with the internal technical team on CRM configuration and dashboards, and with the sales operations team to validate the workflows and run user acceptance testing. My academic supervisor reviewed the work.

The approach

1. One clean record per customer

Predictive analytics is only as good as the data under it, so I started there:

  • Standardised records: names and contact details were cleaned so that the same person entered in two ways became one record.
  • Capture at the point of sale: validation rules ensured future buying occasions were recorded at the moment of purchase. Where an occasion was missing (a bridal purchase with no wedding date, for example), the system created a task for the sales executive to ask during the next conversation.
  • A structured customer ID with family mapping: each ID encodes the customer type, location and gender, with an extension that links relatives. This moved the CRM from an individual view to a household view, which matters in a category where purchases are driven by family occasions.

2. Segmenting customers by value

I used a weighted RFM model (recency, frequency and monetary value) to group customers into high, medium and low value. Recency and monetary value did the most work in a category defined by occasions and high-value purchases, and frequency helped identify loyal advocates who buy around peak seasons.

3. Rules that explain themselves

On top of the segments, three rule-based triggers create tasks automatically:

Milestone

An anniversary or birthday within a year creates a task to invite the customer in for a bespoke design

Lifecycle

After an engagement ring purchase, the system prompts an appointment for a wedding showcase

Dormancy

A top-tier customer not contacted for a year triggers a winback task for the team leader

I chose rules over a black-box model on purpose. A sales executive can see why a customer was flagged, and people act on what they understand. That mattered more than a few points of predictive accuracy.

4. A four-stage operating loop

The insights only count if they change daily work, so I built them into a closed loop: data is captured, analysed, acted on and monitored.

StageWhat it does
1. Sales enablementCustomer profile, family view and occasion tracking in front of the sales executive
2. Analytical reportingConversion, walk-in, follow-up, dormancy, RFM and data-quality reports
3. Task managementReferral and retention programs, pitch prompts and the order pipeline
4. MonitoringChecklists, live dashboards and error-proofing rules

One dataset, many readings

The most useful insight from the project was that every program looks at the same customer data and asks it a different question. If each program built its own view, the business would have five versions of the truth. So I mapped each question to a report or task in the CRM, and tied all of them to one goal: turn occasions into qualified visits, visits into purchases, and purchases into lasting relationships.

WhoThe question they askWhat the framework gives them
Sales teamWhom should I call today, and what should I say?Occasion alerts, pitch prompts, family view, past design preferences for layered cross-selling
Referral program (gift cards)Which customers are likely to bring in qualified referrals?RFM segments and family links to find advocates; referrals tracked through to walk-ins and conversion
Retention program (digital gold)Who stays engaged between purchases, and who is drifting?Engagement and redemption tied to the customer record; dormancy flags for lapsed accounts
Orders and inventoryWhat is coming, and are we ready?Order pipeline milestones, demand signals from upcoming occasions, dispatch alerts once hallmarking clears
LeadershipIs the funnel healthy, and where is it leaking?Conversion, walk-in, follow-up and dormancy reports, with delays flagged on live dashboards

Each reading fed one of four shared measures: walk-in rate (referrals and digital-gold redemptions), conversion rate (occasion-based pitch prompts and style analysis), SOP adherence (mandatory checklists and error-proofing) and customer retention (digital-gold engagement). Because every program reported into the same four measures, leadership could compare them and decide where to invest next.

Dashboards that drive action

I designed the reports around decisions, not around data. Each one answers a question someone has to act on:

ReportPurpose
Conversion reportTrack performance from walk-in to purchase
FJBO alertsSurface future jewellery buying occasions
Data fill rateKeep customer data complete enough to predict from
Walk-in and follow-up reportsAnalyse footfall and track orders and payments
Dormancy and RFM reportsFind inactive customers and rank customers by value

The monitoring layer borrowed from lean management. A task cannot be marked complete until the mandatory details are captured. Live dashboards give management a view of the sales funnel and the custom-order pipeline, and any delay beyond the agreed service level is flagged visually. Error-proofing rules stop mistakes before they happen: a delivery cannot be scheduled until quality check and hallmarking are cleared.

Winning the sales team over

The biggest obstacle was behavioural. Consultants who had worked from personal diaries for years saw the CRM as a tool for watching them. I reframed it as sales enablement: the system remembers every client's important dates so that they can spend their time on the conversation. Combined with hands-on training and quizzes, the team reached full adoption within four weeks.

Results

These are the outcomes of the CRM program this framework was built into:

  • 100% adoption within 4 weeks of go-live
  • +8% conversion rate in Q1 2026, compared with the same quarter last year
  • +15% customer retention through Q2 2026, supported by the referral and retention initiatives
  • +15% process efficiency across data collection, ordering, fulfilment and follow-ups
  • −10% operational errors, driven by program cadence and customer feedback loops
  • A shift from an individual customer view to a family view, with stronger personalisation and cross-selling

A note on the data

The framework was tested on a simulated dataset modelled on real transaction patterns, so no customer or company data appears in this case study. It has limits: the rule-based model can miss unusual cases that a machine-learning model might catch, it was applied in a single organisation, and long-term measures such as customer lifetime value were outside the project timeline.

Reflection

The value of analytics lies less in the model and more in putting its output into the daily workflow. If I did it again, I would keep the transparent rules as the first layer, then test a machine-learning model against them once there is enough live data, and connect the CRM to the ERP so that inventory and demand signals stay in step.
