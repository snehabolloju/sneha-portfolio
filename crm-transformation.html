Digitising client relationships: an end-to-end CRM transformation for a luxury jewellery retailer

Role
Program Manager (Lead – CRM & Talent Management)
Timeline
Nov 2025 – Feb 2026 (Phase 1 go-live), results tracked through Q2 2026
Scale
3 branches · 43 sales staff · 6 vendor teams · Sales, Inventory, Orders and Leadership stakeholders

+15%customer retention through Q2 2026
−10%operational errors

The challenge

In luxury jewellery, client relationships are the business. Yet at this retailer, they lived in salespeople's personal diaries and on their mobile phones, with no common format and no central record.

The effects showed up everywhere:

  • Lost revenue: follow-ups were missed, orders slipped through, and sales opportunities went unclaimed.
  • Inconsistent service: without a client's history, salespeople couldn't build rapport, and design details were sometimes captured wrongly, leading to returns and unhappy customers.
  • Delayed deliveries: order information wasn't consistently recorded or shared.
  • Client loss when staff left: when a salesperson left, their clients often left with them, because there was no structured handover.
  • No leadership visibility: management had little insight into the client base or the sales pipeline.

Leadership recognised the record-keeping problem. When I examined it more closely, I found the deeper gaps: no SOPs, no training framework, and implementation barriers rooted in both skill (unfamiliarity with digital tools) and will (scepticism about changing how they worked). A software tool alone wouldn't fix this. It needed a full program spanning process, technology and people.

My role

As part of the core team and the business-side owner of the program, I was the primary point of contact between the business and six vendor teams: CRM development, gift cards (referral program), digital gold (customer retention), Android and iOS app development, billing services, and campaigns. I owned:

  • Requirements gathering across Sales, Inventory, Orders and Leadership
  • Use cases and user stories for the development teams
  • Feature prioritisation and Phase 1 scoping
  • Vendor coordination, status tracking and escalations
  • User Acceptance Testing (UAT)
  • SOPs, training decks, quizzes, and user support through and after go-live

I worked daily with the Hyderabad sales team (30 people, including 2 floor managers) and drove change management across all 3 branches (43 sales staff in total).

The approach

1. Building requirements from scratch

There was no existing process documentation, so everything had to be built from zero. Over about 30 days, I gathered requirements from sales, inventory, orders and leadership, documented them as use cases and user stories, and prioritised them into 4 major feature areas for Phase 1:

  1. Customer profiles and segmentation: a profile overview, mapping of existing account data, classification of customers and walk-ins as new or existing, and purchase-history-based lifecycle segmentation (RFM)
  2. Follow-ups and order tracking: structured follow-up types with reminders, and end-to-end order tracking
  3. Team productivity and performance: daily task management, productivity mapping, and individual KPIs
  4. Leadership dashboards: real-time visibility into the client base and team performance

2. Bridging business and development

Early on, the development teams struggled to understand why certain features mattered. I addressed this by explaining every key scenario from three angles:

The user

The salesperson entering and using the data

The consumer

The customer experiencing the service

Leadership

How the data would support decisions over the long term

This gave developers the business context behind each requirement and noticeably reduced back-and-forth during the build.

3. Running the program on data

I turned our MIS tracking sheet from a static log into the program's single source of truth, built on four pillars:

  • Delivery and quality: target vs. achievement, feature priorities, sprint rollovers and UAT defects. When features rolled over or defects rose, the data guided re-planning of upcoming sprint commitments based on real team capacity.
  • Blockers and dependencies: I tracked blockers and their age, along with every vendor deliverable, mapping upstream and downstream risks so vendor delays were escalated before they affected the critical path.
  • Program rhythm: tracker updates were linked to scrum calls and office hours, and decisions were shared through written minutes of meetings, which cut down unnecessary status meetings.
  • Operational readiness: SOP versions, training hours and quiz scores were tracked alongside development progress.

My CSM, CSPO and SAFe training shaped how I used it. Making the tracker visible to all stakeholders kept delays from going unnoticed. When defects or rollovers threatened the timeline, I used the data to lead backlog trade-off discussions with business stakeholders. And by cross-checking vendor deliverables against internal timelines, I kept six vendor teams moving as one coordinated release.

4. Adding an analytics layer

As part of my MBA in Business Analytics, I designed an analytics framework on top of the CRM to move the team from reactive to proactive selling. It included RFM segmentation to rank customers by value, and rule-based triggers that create follow-up tasks automatically: for an upcoming milestone occasion, after a lifecycle purchase such as an engagement ring, or when a top-tier customer has been inactive for a year. I chose rule-based logic over a black-box model on purpose, so the sales team could see why each customer was flagged.

5. Redefining "Done"

A feature wasn't considered done when development finished. It was done only when SOPs were published, staff were trained, and they had passed a certification quiz. This kept go-live quality high and meant the business was ready to use each feature from day one.

6. Phased, semi-digital rollout

Rather than a single switch-over, the team began working under the new SOPs before full go-live. Features were used as soon as they went live, and processes not yet built were tracked in Excel in the new structured format. By the third week of February, most features were live, and the team was already used to the new way of working.

7. Winning over the sales team

The hardest part of the program was people, not technology. Many salespeople were sceptical about moving from diaries to a digital platform. Instead of one-way training, I ran interactive sessions with role-play activities, supported by office hours and quick-reference materials. The team performed well in quizzes, and adoption climbed from 15% in week 1 to 40% in week 2, reaching the full team by week 4.

Results

  • 100% adoption within 4 weeks of go-live, from 15% in week 1
  • +8% conversion rate in Q1 2026, compared with the same quarter last year
  • +15% process efficiency across data collection, order placement, fulfilment and follow-ups
  • +15% customer retention through Q2 2026, supported by the referral and retention initiatives launched on the CRM
  • −10% operational errors, driven by program cadence and customer feedback loops
  • Higher follow-up rates, more repeat customers, fewer complaints, and real-time visibility for leadership into the client base

Senior leadership recognised the program's structured approach to identifying and closing operational gaps.

Reflection

The biggest lesson was that digital transformation succeeds or fails on adoption. Involving the sales team earlier, for example by bringing a few senior salespeople into requirements and UAT as champions, would likely have reduced resistance at go-live and sped up adoption.
